The Frank-Wolfe algorithm, also known as the conditional gradient method, is one of the oldest methods for constrained convex optimization.

CONSTRAINED CONVEX OPTIMIZATION PROBLEM: is a mathematical formulation aimed at minimizing an objective function, where the optimization variable x is subject to specific constraints.
•Objective Function (f): The function f to be minimized is convex. It is often assumed to be differentiable and to have a Lipschitz continuous gradient. In certain contexts, it may also be strongly convex. 
For functions with a Lipschitz continuous gradient, the convexity property implies the "Descent Lemma," which provides a quadratic upper approximation of the function f.
•Constraint Set (C, D, or M): The minimization occurs over a feasible set or domain, denoted as C, D, or M. This set must be convex and compact (i.e., bounded and closed). Examples of constraint domains include polytopes such as the standard simplex, 
the L1-ball, the Birkhoff polytope, flow polytopes, and permutahedra.
The primary goal of constrained convex optimization is to find a global solution x* that minimizes f(x) within the feasible domain.

PROPERTIES OF FW ALGORITHM:
1. PROJECTION FREE NATURE,  it does not require costly projections onto the feasible domain. Instead, each iteration only involves minimizing a linear function over the domain, an operation typically referred to as a Linear Minimization Oracle (LMO).
This "projection-free" property can lead to significantly lower per-iteration computational costs compared to projection-based methods, especially for large-scale problems or domains without easy projection. 

2. SPARSITY OF ITERATES, FW promotes sparse and structured solutions.  In each iteration, at most one new "atom" (extreme point of the domain) is added to the current iterate's convex combination. 
For example, if the optimization domain is a simplex, the iterate after k steps can be represented as a sparse convex combination of at most k+1 atoms. This property connects FW to many greedy optimization schemes.

3. APPLICATIONS,  FW methods are well-suited for problems with structured constraints, appearing widely in machine learning, signal processing, and combinatorial optimization  (matrix completion in this case)

CHALLENGE OF CLASSICAL FW CONVERGENCE
The classical FW algorithm is known for its slow, sublinear convergence rate (O(1/t))
This slow-down is particularly pronounced when the optimal solution lies on the boundary of the feasible set, 
leading to a "zig-zagging phenomenon" where the algorithm's iterates oscillate back and forth between vertices defining the face containing the solution.

GLOBAL LINEAR CONVERGENCE
Several popular variants of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm, when correctly implemented, achieve global linear convergence. 
This means their optimization error decreases geometrically with O((1-ρ)^t) instead of O(1/t), which is a significant speed-up.
"Our analysis yields a weaker sufficient condition than Robinson’s condition; in particular we can have linear convergence even in some cases when the function has more than one global minima, 
and is not globally strongly convex. The constant also naturally separates as the product of the condition number of the function with a novel notion of condition number of a polytope, 
which might have applications in complexity theory."

CONSTANT ρ:
It's the product of the classical condition number of the function and a newly defined geometric quantity that serves as a "condition number" of the constraint set.
"L/µ is the standard condition number of the objective function appearing in the rates of gradient methods in convex optimization. 
The second quantity (M/δ)2 (diameter over pyramidal width) can be interpreted as a condition number of the domain M, or its eccentricity. 
The more eccentric the constraint set (large diameter compared to its pyramidal width), the slower the convergence. The best condition number of a function is when its level sets are spherical; 
the analog in term of the constraint sets is actually the regular simplex, 
which has the maximum width-to-diameter ratio amongst all simplices"

There is proves for:
•Away-steps Frank-Wolfe (AFW)
Purpose: Addresses the zig-zagging problem of classical FW.
Mechanism: In addition to moving towards a "good" FW atom (st), AFW introduces the possibility of moving away from a "bad" atom (vt) in the current active set S(t). 
The "away direction" dA_t = x(t) - vt is chosen by finding the atom vt in S(t) that maximizes the potential for descent. 
This search is performed over the (typically small) active set, making it computationally easier than the LMO.
Step Size: The maximum step size (γmax) for an away step ensures that the new iterate remains within the feasible domain M while maintaining a valid convex representation.
Drop Steps: If the line search yields γt = γmax, it's called a "drop step," meaning the atom vt is fully removed from S(t). The number of drop steps is limited (at most t/2 up to iteration t).
Convergence: AFW is proven to converge linearly for strongly convex functions

•Pairwise Frank-Wolfe (PFW)
Inspiration: Based on the MDM algorithm by Mitchell et al..
Mechanism: Instead of allowing a general "away" step that might affect all weights in the active set, PFW specifically moves weight mass only between the current FW atom (st) and the chosen away atom (vt).
Sparsity & Performance: Unlike classical FW, which shrinks all active weights, PFW is often more effective at pruning coordinates from the active set S(t). 
It frequently outperforms AFW in practice, especially for problems with sparse solutions.
Swap Steps: PFW can experience "swap steps" where the mass is entirely transferred from an away atom to an FW atom, but the size of the active set remains unchanged. 
The theoretical bound on the number of these problematic swap steps is very loose (3|A|!)

•Fully-Corrective Frank-Wolfe (FCFW)
Mechanism: This variant performs more intensive work between LMO calls. After finding a new FW atom st, it re-optimizes the objective function f over the entire "correction polytope" conv(A(t)), 
which is typically the convex hull of all previously selected atoms.
Nomenclature: Also known as the extended FW method or simplicial decomposition.
Convergence: The paper proves global linear convergence for FCFW, even if the correction step is only approximately solved, provided it guarantees at least as much progress as a standard FW step. 
A notable property is that FCFW does not have "drop steps" as it effectively performs both FW and away steps simultaneously to ensure sufficient progress

•Wolfe’s Minimum Norm Point (MNP) algorithm
Relationship to FCFW: Often confused with FCFW, especially for quadratic objectives. 
Standard FCFW opti-mizes f over conv(A(t)), whereas MNP implements the correction as a sequence of affine projec-tions on the active set that potentially yield a different update.
Mechanism: MNP implements the correction step as a sequence of affine projections on the active set, which can be computationally more efficient in some practical applications.
Key Differences from FCFW: After a correction, MNP ensures x(t+1) minimizes f over the affine hull of A(t+1). Also, the active atoms A(t) for MNP are always affinely independent and identical to the active set S(t), unlike FCFW which can use redundant or inactive atoms.
Convergence: While prior analyses only showed finite convergence (with potentially exponential bounds on iterations), this paper is the first to provide global linear convergence rates for MNP for general strongly convex functions. 
MNP can also have drop steps.

ANALYSIS OF GLOBAL LINEAR CONVERGENCE FOR THE VARIANTS 
- Intuition from Guélat and Marcotte: Linear convergence relies on ensuring that the "angle" between the update direction dt and the negative gradient rt = -∇f(x(t)) remains bounded away from zero. 
Classical FW's zig-zagging causes this angle to approach zero, leading to sublinear rates.
- The Pairwise FW Direction's Role: A key insight for AFW's linear convergence is the relationship 2〈rt,dt〉 ≥ 〈rt,dPFW_t〉, where dPFW_t = st - vt is the pairwise FW direction. 
This inequality ensures that the descent is sufficiently large.
- Pyramidal Width (PWidth(A)): This novel geometric quantity is crucial for bounding the inner product of the negative gradient and the pairwise FW direction away from zero.
◦It's defined as the minimum "pyramidal directional width" across all faces of conv(A) and all feasible directions.
◦Examples: For the unit cube in Rd, PWidth(A) = 1/√d. For the probability simplex with d vertices, PWidth(A) is 2/√d (for even d) or 2/√d - 1/d (for odd d)
- The Linear Convergence Rate (ρf): The suboptimality ht = f(x(t)) - f(x*) decreases geometrically as ht+1 ≤ (1-ρf)ht for "good steps" (i.e., when γt < γmax). 
The constant ρf elegantly combines the classical condition number of the function (µ/L, where µ is the strong convexity constant and L is the Lipschitz constant of the gradient) with a new geometric "condition number" of the constraint set ((Pyramidal Width / Diameter)^2)
- Handling "Bad Steps": While "good steps" ensure linear progress, some iterations (e.g., "drop steps" in AFW/MNP, or "swap steps" in PFW) might not show sufficient progress immediately. 
The paper provides bounds on the frequency of these "bad steps." For AFW and MNP, the number of drop steps is at most t/2. FCFW inherently avoids drop steps by guaranteeing sufficient progress. 
For PFW, the number of swap steps can be very large in theory (up to 3|A|!).
- Sublinear Convergence for General Convex Objectives: When the objective function is not strongly convex (µ = 0), all four variants still achieve a sublinear convergence rate of O(1/k(t)), where k(t) is the number of "good steps" taken.

AFFINE INVARIANCE
This means that the algorithm's behavior and its convergence constants remain unchanged under affine transformations of the problem. 
This property is vital because optimization performance often heavily depends on the "distortion" of the domain in other methods, while FW is immune to such effects.
- Curvature Constant (Cf): Used as an affine-invariant measure of the objective function's non-linearity. It quantifies how much f deviates from its linear approximation. 
If ∇f is L-Lipschitz, Cf ≤ L * diam(M)^2. The variant CA_f is defined for away-steps to account for points possibly outside the domain during line search.
- Geometric Strong Convexity (µA_f): This is an affine-invariant notion of strong convexity that depends on the geometry of the domain. It plays a similar role to the standard strong convexity constant µ in non-affine-invariant analyses.

GENERALIZATION TO NON STRONGLY CONVEX FUNCTIONS 
The paper extends the linear convergence results to a more general class of functions f(x) := g(Ax) + <b,x>, where g is strongly convex but f itself is not necessarily strongly convex. 
This generalization introduces a "generalized strong convexity constant" (µ̃ or µ̃f) linked to the Hoffman constant, and the linear convergence rates are shown to hold with µA_f replaced by µ̃f

EMPIRICAL VALIDATION
The theoretical findings are supported by numerical experiments on problems like constrained Lasso and video co-localization. These experiments demonstrate that AFW and PFW empirically outperform the classical FW algorithm and indeed exhibit linear convergence. 
The theoretical convergence rate constant for AFW and PFW is shown to be empirically quite tight, particularly for domains with very acute angles.
